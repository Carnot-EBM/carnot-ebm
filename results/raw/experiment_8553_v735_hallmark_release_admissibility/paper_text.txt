Rule- and Large Language Model (LLM) -based verifiers are emerging, but many are released without a shared evaluation and each flags a different kind of error, so it is unclear which to trust or when to deploy them. We address this with Hallmark ( Hall ucination bench mark ): 2,526 BibTeX entries spanning 14 hallucination types, with per-type and diagnostic sub-test labels and further temporal, cross-domain, and real-world evaluation splits. We evaluate zero-shot and tool-augmented LLMs , a DOI -lookup baseline, and our own co-designed rule-based verifier, bibtex-updater . Verifiers can miss hallucinations and wrongly flag correct papers, so every flag still needs human review. The false positive rate (FPR) determines the review effort and thus limits deployment. The tested models exhibit an order-of-magnitude spread in FPR ; agentic lookups buy recall but inflate FPR , and neither smaller models nor prompt optimization reach the frontier’s operating point. A two-stage cascade with bibtex-updater avoids that inflation, providing an upper bound, given the co-design. These rankings, however, depend on what counts as a hallucination: re-scoring the types that describe a real work cited inaccurately changes which verifier ranks first, and venues have not agreed on a definition. We call for a community definition and release per-type labels and a re-scoring protocol so that any agreed definition can be scored against every verifier. † † footnotetext: Benchmark and code: https://github.com/rpatrik96/hallmark ; verification tool ( bibtex-updater ): https://github.com/rpatrik96/bibtexupdater . 1 Introduction Hallucinated citations—fabricated references generated by language models—have become a prevalent threat to scientific publishing, especially because they often seem plausible. Shortly after NeurIPS 2025, GPTZero audited the 4,841 accepted papers and found 53 with fabricated citations that had passed peer review ( Shmatko et al., 2026 ; Ansari, 2026 ) , and independent audits report hundreds more across venues and a sharp rise since 2021 ( Xu et al., 2026 ; Sakai et al., 2026a ; Bienz et al., 2026 ) . Venues now screen submissions for them: ICLR 2026 ran an automated reference checker, desk-rejected papers with confirmed hallucinations, and reported a significant false-positive rate ( ICLR, 2026 ) . Verifiers have multiplied, from DOI resolvers to multi-database cross-referencing systems and LLM -based checkers. Many are released without a shared evaluation, and often focus on different error types, ranging from records that cannot be found to mismatched authors, venue or year. It is unclear which to trust, or how to deploy them. To enable comparisons and to provide practical guidance on deployment for authors and venues, we release Hallmark , comprising 2,526 BibTeX entries spanning 14 hallucination types across three difficulty tiers, with taxonomy labels and six diagnostic sub-tests. We evaluate a DOI -lookup baseline, frontier and small LLMs zero-shot, tool-augmented agents, and our own rule-based verifier bibtex-updater , which was co-designed with the taxonomy and is therefore reported as an upper bound rather than ranked. We score every verifier under a single protocol ( Fig. 1 ) and rely on both a held-out test set and four additional evaluation-only splits that add recent papers, a second domain, and authentic ChatGPT citations. Scope. Hallmark targets citation metadata —detecting fabricated, inconsistent, or nonexistent bibliographic records—not claim-level hallucination, which needs full-text analysis. Current citation verifiers are not perfect; thus, human review is required, and the false-positive rate, not recall, becomes the bottleneck for deployment , as we show in our positive predictive value (PPV) analysis in § 4.4 , with FPR spanning an order of magnitude ( 0.043 0.043 to 0.702 0.702 ). We observe and analyze three failure modes ( Tab. 20 ): (i) agentic lookups buy recall but inflate FPR ; (ii) precision collapses at realistic prevalence; and (iii) most LLMs by default over-flag papers published after their training cutoff. Some of these failure modes can be mitigated: a two-stage cascade with bibtex-updater avoids inflating FPR by passing only uncertain entries to an LLM . It catches nearly all hallucinations at a low false-positive rate, which we treat as an upper bound given the co-design. A prompt that reminds the model of its training cutoff (i.e., cutoff-aware) reduces over-flagging of recent papers, but mostly through abstention; mitigating it selectively is left for future work. Prompt optimization ( Agrawal et al., 2026 ) also has limits, especially for smaller models, which maintain high FPR . Verifier performance and ranking depend on the—perhaps surprisingly ambiguous—definition of a hallucination. For example, a real work cited with the wrong venue is not fully correct, but is that a hallucination? Existing verifiers address this differently in what they check, and venues have not (yet) reached consensus. We therefore urge the community to have more discussion on definitions of hallucinated citations; meanwhile, we release per-type labels and a re-scoring protocol so that any agreed definition can be scored against every verifier. Our contributions are: 1. A benchmark and taxonomy for citation-hallucination detection, with per-type and sub-test labels and temporal, cross-domain, and real-world splits ( § 3 ). 2. A failure-mode analysis of citation verifiers under one protocol, showing that the false-positive rate decides deployability ( § 4.4 ). 3. A re-scoring protocol that lets any definition of a hallucinated citation be applied to every verifier. 2 Related work Citation hallucination in the LLM era. LLM hallucination ( Ji et al., 2023 ; Huang et al., 2025 ) manifests distinctly in citations ( Alkaissi & McFarlane, 2023 ; Agrawal et al., 2024 ; Walters & Wilder, 2023 ) ; venue-level audits document scale—GhostCite ( Xu et al., 2026 ) on 56K papers, HalluCitation ( Sakai et al., 2026a ) on ACL/NAACL/EMNLP, the HPC-venue study of Bienz et al. (2026) , and CITADEL’s audit of 2.5 million PubMed Central papers ( Topaz et al., 2026 ) —measuring prevalence , not detection-tool performance. Detection tools—HaRC ( HaRC Contributors, 2024 ) , verify-citations ( verify-citations Contributors, 2025 ) , RefChecker ( Hu et al., 2024 ) , CheckIfExist ( Abbonato, 2026 ) —report incompatible metrics on disjoint test data, preventing direct comparison; CheckIfExist (a CrossRef/Semantic-Scholar/OpenAlex cascade verifier) ships without dataset or metrics, and RefChecker requires full-text manuscript context. Concurrent work ( Rao & Callison-Burch, 2026 ; Rao et al., 2026 ; Sakai et al., 2026b ) evaluates LLM -as-citation-generator accuracy or releases lightweight checkers. The closest work to Hallmark is CiteAudit ( Shi et al., 2026 ) , which likewise benchmarks LLMs and commercial verifiers on a human-validated/annotated dataset via a multi-agent pipeline. Hallmark differs by focusing on understanding failure modes by rich diagnostics. It provides a 14-type, tier-stratified error taxonomy and post-cutoff evaluation splits that expose training-cutoff effects, so it reports which metadata failures each tool catches. Adjacent concurrent detection work ( Rao et al., 2026 ) targets citation-URL liveness on DRBench (the stale-vs-hallucinated distinction), a scope orthogonal to Hallmark ’s BibTeX-metadata cross-consistency checks. § C.7 details boundary cases of audit-vs-benchmark categorization. Hallucination detection benchmarks. General-purpose hallucination benchmarks ( Li et al., 2023 ; Ravichander et al., 2025 ) target factual claims, not metadata; adjacent work—FactScore ( Min et al., 2023 ) , RARR ( Gao et al., 2023 ) , SciFact ( Wadden et al., 2020 ) —does not evaluate BibTeX integrity ( Digital Object Identifier (DOI) /title/author/venue cross-consistency against external databases). Concurrent generator-side benchmarks such as HalluHard ( Fan et al., 2026 ) measure whether LLMs produce claims grounded by retrievable inline citations in multi-turn dialogs. None evaluates the verifiers that check citation metadata. Hallmark ’s design borrows from established benchmarks: temporal segmentation, as in SWE-bench ( Jimenez et al., 2024 ) and LiveCodeBench ( Jain et al., 2025 ) , separates recall from verification, and multi-criteria sub-tests, as in HumanEval ( Chen et al., 2021 ) , attribute each failure to a field ( § C.7 ). 3 The Hallmark benchmark 3.1 Hallucination taxonomy We define 14 citation hallucination types—11 empirically grounded and 3 stress-test types—organized into three difficulty tiers based on the verification effort required ( Tab. 2 , in the appendix). Tier 1 (Easy) hallucinations are settled by one check, a single lookup ( fabricated_doi , nonexistent_venue ) or none at all ( placeholder_authors , future_date ). Tier 2 (Medium) hallucinations require cross-referencing multiple metadata fields: e.g., chimeric_title pairs real authors with a fabricated title, wrong_venue assigns a paper to the wrong conference, and hybrid_fabrication uses a real DOI whose resolved record does not match the BibTeX metadata. Tier 3 (Hard) hallucinations require deep verification or semantic reasoning: near_miss_title entries differ by one or two words from a real paper; plausible_fabrication entries are entirely invented but realistic; and arxiv_version_mismatch entries cite a preprint with wrong venue and shifted year. The tiers rank the effort a human verifier needs, not difficulty for an LLM . The taxonomy is grounded in real incidents: we derived it from hallucinated citations in the NeurIPS 2025 incident and related audits ( Xu et al., 2026 ; Sakai et al., 2026a ) , then stress-tested it with adversarial brainstorming of failure modes existing tools might miss. Of the 108 real-world hallucinated citations in the benchmark, the 72 we analyzed in detail map cleanly to a single type 79% of the time, with the rest assigned to their highest applicable tier ( § A.2 ). Three types— merged_citation , partial_author_list , arxiv_version_mismatch —we could not trace to a documented incident, so we treat them as stress-test types. They appear in every split (18.3% of hallucinated entries on dev_public , 20.2% on test_public ); per-type labels let each type be scored separately. Perturbation rules, generation prompts and the collection procedure are in §§ A.3 and B.2.3 . 3.2 Dataset construction The dataset contains two classes of entries: valid references scraped from DBLP and hallucinated references built with the four methods below. Valid entries. We scraped BibTeX records from DBLP ( dblp Team, 2025 ) for papers published at major ML venues (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, AAAI, CVPR) between 2021 and 2023, plus 35 arXiv preprints from February 2026 (22 dev_public , 13 test_public ) as a recency probe. Each entry was verified by confirming DOI resolution where a DOI is present ( 46 % 46\% of valid entries), title existence in at least two databases, and author-venue consistency. This retained 1,036 valid entries. Hallucinated entries. We generated hallucinated entries using four methods: Systematic perturbation : modifying specific fields of valid entries to produce targeted hallucination types (e.g., replacing a DOI with a non-resolving one for fabricated_doi , swapping author lists between papers for swapped_authors ). LLM generation : prompting language models to generate plausible but fictional references for types requiring coherent fabrication ( plausible_fabrication , chimeric_title ). Adversarial crafting : manually constructing entries designed to evade specific detection strategies. Real-world collection : harvesting actual hallucinated citations from published papers identified in audits. An evaluation-only supplement of 341 authentic ChatGPT-generated citations (172 valid / 169 hallucinated), hand-verified by Walters & Wilder (2023) across 42 multidisciplinary topics, adds real-world hallucinations ( § C.6 ). Labeling. Labels are assigned deterministically by the generation pipeline; LLM -generated entries are also filtered against bibliographic databases, and real-world and adversarial entries receive manual review ( § A.3 ). Splits and release. Every hallucinated entry is built by modifying some real base paper . Across the two public splits those base papers do not overlap: no hallucinated entry on one split is built from a paper that appears as a valid entry on the other. test_hidden and stress_test do not have this property: 109 of their 244 and 68 of their 121 hallucinated entries modify a paper that is a valid entry in public data. Most types on dev_public and test_public carry ≥ 30 \geq 30 instances, enough for meaningful per-type comparison; test_hidden spreads 244 hallucinated entries over 14 types and does not clear that floor, so its per-type intervals are wider (statistical power in § B.1 ). Every entry passes automated validation, the url field is blinded to avoid using URL resolution as a shortcut for verification, unique marker tokens in the metadata expose memorization if a model reproduces one, and types are roughly balanced to maximize per-type power ( § A.3 ). The core splits are dev_public (1,119 entries), test_public (831), test_hidden (454), and stress_test (122); per-split counts and the four evaluation-only splits are in Tab. 5 . Entries carry a temporal segment tag ( pre-2023 , 2023–2024 , 2025+ ), used for the post-cutoff analysis in §§ 4.4 and F . Sub-test design. To understand what is hallucinated and why , each entry decomposes citation validity into six independently verifiable dimensions, each scored True/False/N/A: DOI resolves , Title exists in bibliographic databases, Authors match the identified paper, Venue real and correctly attributed, Fields complete , Cross-DB agreement . The six sub-tests also make an entry’s defect count observable: one type can span several failed sub-tests, and multi-defect entries are the norm in the real-world subset, whereas under perturbation they are rare. Detection by defect count is in § C.5.3 . 4 Experiments 4.1 Metrics Our comparison focuses on two prevalence-independent metrics, ensuring they remain comparable across splits with differing class ratios. Detection rate (DR) is recall on the hallucinated class; FPR is the fraction of valid entries wrongly flagged. Tier-weighted F1 (TW-F1) weights each hallucinated entry by its tier (1/2/3), rewarding the detection of harder hallucinations. Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) reads all four confusion-matrix cells; it moves with prevalence by ≤ 0.03 {\leq}0.03 between our splits ( 54.2 % 54.2\% vs. 62.5 % 62.5\% hallucinated). Expected Calibration Error (ECE) ( Naeini et al., 2015 ) measures how well a tool’s confidence tracks its accuracy; an ECE below 0.05 0.05 is excellent and above 0.2 0.2 unreliable. Formal definitions are in § B.1 ; per-tier and per-type breakdowns in §§ 4.3 and C.4 . 4.2 Evaluated tools We evaluate thirteen independent full-coverage tools (i.e., returning a verdict on every entry, neither co-designed with the benchmark nor given tool access) on dev_public : a DOI -only lower bound and twelve zero-shot LLMs from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Mistral, DeepSeek and Qwen ( Tab. 1 ; model IDs in § B.2.4 ), including a later-cutoff GPT-5.4 control (Aug 2025 cutoff) for § 4.4 . On top of these, four agentic variants ( § B.2.4 ) allow up to five database lookups per entry; prompted to cross-reference databases, the model tends to flag an entry as soon as one lookup returns no match—the disposition behind the inflated false-positive rate we report in § 4.4 . The DOI -only baseline only verifies each entry’s DOI against the DOI.org resolver. Among independent tools, Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.7 give the best F1 and calibration at a middling FPR , a ranking that survives a prompt-paraphrase ablation ( § H.2 ). 4.4 Failure modes Three failure modes recur across the cohort ( Tab. 20 ); we diagnose each in turn. Agentic lookups give diminishing returns (failure mode (i)). Five tool calls push GPT-5.1’s recall past bibtex-updater ’s, but its false-positive rate rises to roughly nine times bibtex-updater ’s, because the prompted model—cross-referencing against CrossRef/OpenAlex/arXiv—tends to flag an entry as soon as one of them returns no match. The inflation is a property of the harness—its naive, single-stage use of the lookups—not of the base model or of tool access itself: swapping Sonnet 4.6 for GPT-5.1 reproduces the profile within ≤ 5 {\leq}5 pp on every metric, and Sonnet’s FPR rises ∼ 3.4 × {\sim}3.4{\times} over its zero-shot baseline from the same collection, while GPT-5.1’s already-high zero-shot FPR barely changes. This any-no-match policy fails because CrossRef, OpenAlex, and arXiv have partial, non-overlapping coverage: a real paper is routinely missing from one of them, so single-source absence gets read as fabrication. A better alternative is to flag only on consensus absence across sources (or on positive metadata inconsistency) and to route single-source absence to uncertain /human review ( § E.2 ); § 4.5 measures that policy. A deterministic re-aggregation over three of the harness’s four databases confirms improvement ( Appx. D ), so failure mode (i) characterizes a retrieval-augmented pattern, not an inherent shortcoming of agentic systems. Tool access therefore trades precision for recall: agentic harnesses flag an entry as soon as one database misses it. Precision at low prevalence (failure mode (ii)). Precision ( PPV , the fraction of flags that are genuine hallucinations) falls at low prevalence by Bayes’ rule ( § E.1 ). Our analysis focuses on how verifiers compare, given the wide range of FPR in Tab. 1 , from 0.043 0.043 to 0.702 0.702 . At a venue-realistic ∼ 2 % {\sim}2\% base rate that becomes a ∼ 6 × {\sim}6{\times} gap in precision: the best verifiers catch one true hallucination per 6–10 flags, the most aggressive fewer than one in 35, even at ≥ 87 % {\geq}87\% detection (per-tool PPV in Tab. 23 , sweep in Tab. 24 ). Calibration does not simply follow FPR : Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.7 are the best-calibrated zero-shot LLMs ( ECE 0.041 0.041 , 0.052 0.052 ) at a middling FPR , while the lowest- FPR model, Gemini 2.5 Pro, is among the worst calibrated ( 0.297 0.297 ). The co-designed bibtex-updater has a low FPR because it flags conservatively rather than abstaining, and reaches one-in-four precision ( § E.2 ). The optional pre-screening layer adds ∼ 5 {\sim}5 pp overall ( ∼ 18 {\sim}18 pp on Tier 1); we report numbers with and without pre-screening. Every metric from which abstentions are excluded is paired with its coverage and an aggressive re-scoring that scores every abstention as hallucinated ( § E.2 ), so deferring hard cases cannot improve low precision. Prevalence (the base rate of hallucinated entries) moves deployability while leaving the ranking fixed. Sweeping the base rate over 1 1 – 5 % 5\% leaves the PPV ordering essentially fixed ( Tab. 24 ), because prevalence cancels from pairwise comparisons. Thus, the DR / FPR ratio determines the ranking; however, the base rate affects absolute precision: at a 2 % 2\% base rate even the best independent verifier (Gemini 2.5 Pro) reaches only ∼ 16 % {\sim}16\% PPV . Still, depending on prevalence, the cost ratio c FN / c FP c_{\text{FN}}/c_{\text{FP}} , and reviewer capacity, it might be worth ranking based on recall. We discuss the different deployment considerations and regimes in § E.3 . Takeaway. Whether a verifier is deployable depends mostly on its FPR , as checking (falsely) flagged entries requires human oversight. At venue-realistic prevalence, precision is set by the ratio of detection rate to false-positive rate. In our experiments, FPR spans an order of magnitude, which substantially influences precision ( PPV ), and thus, whether deployment is feasible. Temporal robustness (failure mode (iii)). A separate 448-entry supplement disjoint from the 2021–2023 corpus (300 valid 2024–2025 DBLP entries, 148 hallucinated; § F.1 ) shows LLM verifiers degrade sharply beyond their training cutoff. The earlier-cutoff models default to “flag everything unfamiliar”: F1 falls to ≈ 0.55 {\approx}0.55 as false positives inflate, with GPT-5.1’s FPR alone rising 41.1 % → 75.9 % 41.1\%{\to}75.9\% . Only the two latest-cutoff Anthropic models hold their false-positive rate near in-distribution levels (Sonnet 4.6 12.0 % 12.0\% , Opus 4.7 7.3 % 7.3\% ); Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT-5.4 ( Tab. 31 ) over-flag only moderately, and the remaining eight degrade sharply to 59.5 59.5 – 88.7 % 88.7\% (the “over-flagging cluster”; Tab. 28 ). Both figures in that comparison come from the May experiment, since a shift across the cutoff is only interpretable within one endpoint snapshot; the September re-run of these two models reported in Tab. 1 puts their dev_public FPR higher ( .161 .161 , .154 .154 ), a drift larger than the temporal shift itself (we provide more details about endpoint drift in Tab. 29 ). We report this as a descriptive pattern, not a calibration result, because the design cannot cleanly separate two explanations: the valid pool is scraped from DBLP and these are the latest-cutoff models in the cohort, so a low FPR on a valid entry is indistinguishable from training-data recall of that same DBLP record; the risk of such contamination—benchmark entries appearing in a model’s training corpus—grows with capability, as stronger models train on larger, newer corpora ( Rank et al., 2026 ) . Two controls, a recall probe and a third-provider late-cutoff model, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, add nuance but do not eliminate the memorization reading; with two models from one provider and a single third-party control, it remains a preliminary signal ( § F.4 ). Takeaway. The two latest-cutoff models keep their post-cutoff FPR low while earlier-cutoff models degrade sharply, and the one third-provider late-cutoff control we ran (DeepSeek-V4-Pro) stays close to Sonnet 4.6. For Opus 4.7, a recall probe points to calibration rather than memorization. We read this as a preliminary descriptive signal, not a causal finding: it rests on a single third-party control, the Anthropic runs have not yet been reproduced on Anthropic’s native API (only through the OpenRouter mirror), and the recall probe relies on whether a model can name the authors from the title alone. 4.5 Stage-2 diagnosis cascade We compose bibtex-updater (Stage 1) with each of Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.1 and GPT-5.4 (Stage 2; up to five tool calls per entry) into a cascade. Stage 1 emits verified , a typed hallucinated verdict, or uncertain ; only the uncertain entries are forwarded to Stage 2. Since all cascade results use bibtex-updater , the co-design caveat applies ( § G.2 ). If we treat the entries that neither of the two stages resolves as hallucinated, then on dev_public the Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.4 cascades both reach DR 0.990 0.990 , at FPR 0.037 0.037 and 0.039 0.039 ( Tab. 1 ); the test_public and stress_test results, and the full panel are in § D.1 . The two-stage cascade can therefore be considered a constructive resolution of the false-positive inflation behind failure mode (i). Takeaway. The cascade is the high-recall configuration at a false-positive rate far below the single-stage harnesses’: Stage 2 returns a verdict on roughly half of Stage 1’s uncertain bucket ( § D.1 ), and the residue is either kept as abstentions ( conservative ) or promoted to a flag ( aggressive ), which lifts recall well above standalone bibtex-updater ’s for two to six points of FPR ( Tab. 22 ). Choose conservative for the lowest FPR ; aggressive somewhat improves recall on harder-tier entries, but the increased FPR requires more manual checks. 5 Discussion Limitations. Hallmark scores verifiers separately on 14 hallucination types, 6 sub-tests and 3 difficulty tiers, but our analysis has limitations, ordered here by potential influence (full discussion in Appx. I ). Because bibtex-updater was co-designed with the taxonomy, it and the cascades built on it should be read as an upper bound on performance ( § G.2 ), and the FPR -driven deployment bottleneck assumes a venue audit limited by reviewer time ( § 4.4 ). Most hallucinated entries are synthetic perturbations, and the 108 real-world entries are type-skewed and rated by an automated (LLM) proxy ( § H.6 ). The core splits are English BibTeX from five ML venues, 2021–2023, supplemented by evaluation-only splits with post-cutoff papers, biomedical and non-ML CS venues, and ChatGPT citations on 42 topics ( § A.3 ). The post-cutoff resistance of the two latest-cutoff models is descriptive, since recency, provider and contamination are confounded ( § F.4 ), and hosted- LLM rows are dated snapshots, susceptible to endpoint drift ( Tab. 29 ). Extended-thinking variants were only tested on 100 entries, where larger budgets did not improve F1 once outputs stopped being truncated ( § F.5 ), and HaRC and verify-citations are excluded because rate limits and blocked lookups keep either from returning its own verdicts at benchmark scale ( § B.2.2 ). Rankings also depend on the definition of a hallucination, which is set outside the benchmark: counting flags on the four types that cite a real work with wrong details as false accusations puts bibtex-updater ahead of the cascade ( § H.1 ). Conclusion. Despite these limitations, Hallmark gives citation verifiers a shared test and one protocol that separates three failure modes ( Tab. 20 ), which helps venues and individual authors decide whether to deploy them. Our experiments suggest that careful design is required to avoid brittleness caused by, e.g., post-cutoff papers or agentic lookups. The two-stage cascade ( § 4.5 ) points to a promising direction; since its first stage is our co-designed tool, testing the design with other first-stage verifiers is left for future work. Finally, Hallmark raises a foundational question for the research community: what counts as a hallucinated citation? AI use statement Generative-AI tools were used in this work as follows. Among the tasks for which disclosure is required, LLMs generated part of the synthetic data set, namely a subset of the hallucinated entries (controlled plausible fabrications and chimeric titles) generated with GPT-5.1, plus ten plausible_fabrication entries generated with GPT-4o-mini, described in § 3.2 (prompts in § B.2.3 ), and served as the three automated raters of the reliability proxy in § H.6 . Ethics statement Hallmark supports scientific integrity by enabling systematic comparison of citation-verification tools, informing venue deployment as LLM -assisted writing becomes standard. A taxonomy of hallucination types could theoretically aid adversaries, but the defensive value of understanding these types outweighs the risk; the taxonomy is derived from publicly documented incidents, not novel attack research. Hallmark is open-source (MIT), GPU-free, and publicly available at https://github.com/rpatrik96/hallmark , ensuring access regardless of institutional resources. The benchmark contains bibliographic metadata only, which is already public: it holds no personal data beyond author names as they appear in published records, and its real-world hallucinated entries come from audits that are themselves public. A flag from any verifier is an accusation against a named author, and at the prevalence of real submissions most flags are false alarms ( § 5 ); we therefore recommend verifier output as a prompt for human review rather than as a finding, and the released tool routes single-source absence to review instead of flagging it. Appendix A Dataset and taxonomy A.1 Full taxonomy details Each hallucination type has a characteristic failure signature ( Tab. 2 ), enabling diagnostic analysis of why a tool succeeds or fails. What is a hallucination? The question seems almost trivial. But looking into the details exposes a lot of nuance: telling what is not a hallucination is easy (a bit-by-bit match with a trusted database entry), but the reverse is not. Different spellings, handling of diacritics, name conventions (e.g., how Asian names get represented in Latin alphabets), hyphenation rules, and changing author lists across (preprint) versions complicate the picture and do not afford a clear-cut decision. With these caveats in mind, the main goal of Hallmark is to analyze potential failure modes without being too pedantic about unconditionally enforcing bit-by-bit matches where humans can also err, even with the best of intentions. This does not mean tool designers should not strive for perfection, but they need to acknowledge these nuances—especially the ones that have a large effect on deployment. The taxonomy spans 14 hallucination types across three lookup-difficulty tiers, ordered by the verification effort each type demands. A.3 Construction details DBLP scraping. Valid entries were scraped from the DBLP API ( dblp.org/search/publ/api ) using venue-specific queries for NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, AAAI, and CVPR. We retrieved BibTeX records, verified DOI resolution via CrossRef, and confirmed title existence in Semantic Scholar. Entries failing any verification step were excluded. Perturbation pipeline. Systematic perturbations follow deterministic rules per hallucination type: • fabricated_doi : Replace DOI with a non-resolving DOI using one of 20 non-existent prefixes and four suffix styles (path, identifier, year-indexed, and conference-indexed) to avoid template-detectable patterns. • nonexistent_venue : Replace venue with an LLM -generated plausible but nonexistent conference name. • placeholder_authors : Replace author list with common placeholder names. • future_date : Set year to current year + 5. • chimeric_title : Keep authors from paper A, replace title with LLM -generated plausible title. • wrong_venue : Keep all fields but swap venue with a different real venue. • swapped_authors : Keep title and venue, replace authors with those from a different paper. • preprint_as_published : Take an arXiv-only paper and add a fabricated venue field. • hybrid_fabrication : Keep a valid DOI but replace title and authors with fabricated metadata. • near_miss_title : Modify 1–2 words via six strategies: synonym substitution (POS-safe pairs), plural/singular flipping, British/American spelling swap, abbreviation expansion/contraction (e.g., “RL” ↔ \leftrightarrow “Reinforcement Learning”), hyphenation toggling (e.g., “self-supervised” ↔ \leftrightarrow “self supervised”), and article removal. • plausible_fabrication : Template-based combinatorial generation of a complete, realistic but nonexistent entry (random author combinations, plausible titles, real venues). A separate set of 113 LLM -generated entries ( Tab. 15 ) resembles what an LLM actually emits, but is generated a different way. • merged_citation : Combine metadata from 2–3 real papers into one entry (e.g., authors from paper A, title from paper B, venue from paper C). • partial_author_list : Take a real paper and drop one or more middle co-authors, keeping only the first and last. • arxiv_version_mismatch : Cite an arXiv preprint with a reassigned venue and shifted publication year. Classified as a stress-test type since no real-world instances have been observed. LLM-generated entries. We prompted GPT-5.1 to generate plausible but fictional citations for types requiring coherent fabrication ( plausible_fabrication , chimeric_title , fabricated_doi ). Each prompt requested a BibTeX entry with realistic metadata for a specified ML venue and year range. Generated entries were verified against CrossRef and DBLP: entries with title similarity ≥ 85 % \geq 85\% (token-sort ratio) and author Jaccard similarity ≥ 0.5 \geq 0.5 to any real paper were flagged as potential duplicates of real work and excluded. This filtering removed approximately 12% of generated entries, documenting an LLM recall failure rate where the model reproduces real papers rather than fabricating new ones. The remaining entries were assigned sub-test labels based on their hallucination type’s expected failure pattern. Real-world collection. We harvested 72 hallucinated citations from three documented incident studies: GPTZero’s NeurIPS 2025 analysis, GhostCite ( Xu et al., 2026 ) , and HalluCitation ( Sakai et al., 2026a ) . Each entry was mapped to the closest taxonomy type based on its failure mode (e.g., a citation with a non-resolving DOI was classified as fabricated_doi ; a citation to a non-existent paper with plausible metadata was classified as plausible_fabrication ). The real-world sample is type-skewed: 55% are plausible_fabrication , reflecting the predominant LLM failure mode in practice. Types without real-world examples ( merged_citation , partial_author_list , arxiv_version_mismatch ) appear in every core split alongside the 11 empirical types; the stress_test split adds evaluation depth on them ( § 3.1 ). Adversarial crafting. We manually constructed entries designed to evade specific detection strategies. These include entries with DOIs that resolve to unrelated papers ( hybrid_fabrication ), entries combining metadata from multiple real papers ( merged_citation ), and entries with plausible but non-existent venues chosen to be close to real venue names. Adversarial entries stress-test tool robustness beyond what template-based perturbation achieves. Quality control. Every generated entry passes through automated validation: (1) BibTeX well-formedness check (valid syntax, and the required fields for the entry’s own type: author/title/journal/year for @article , booktitle for @inproceedings , publisher for @book , school for theses, institution for @techreport , and author/title/year for @misc , with journal waived for an @article carrying an arXiv identifier), (2) sub-test label consistency (sub-test ground truth matches the hallucination type’s expected failure pattern), (3) cross-validation with the valid entry pool to prevent accidental duplicates. Its valid half pairs 15 papers from 2024 with 15 from 2026, the latter scraped from arXiv ML categories via the arXiv API ( scripts/probe_temporal_robustness.py ); its hallucinated half (18 from 2024, 12 from 2026) combines 21 perturbation and 9 adversarial entries. Base-paper overlap across splits. Perturbation entries keep the fields they do not corrupt, so a hallucinated entry can be traced to its base paper by DOI or normalized title. On the public partition the bases are disjoint: 0 of 606 dev_public hallucinated entries match a test_public valid entry, 0 of 519 the other way, the two valid pools share no entry, and two base papers are perturbed in both splits. The auxiliary splits are not disjoint from the public pools: 109 of 244 test_hidden hallucinated entries match a valid entry in dev_public (70) or test_public (39), three test_hidden valid entries duplicate dev_public ones, and 109 of 121 stress_test entries match a valid entry in test_public (64), test_hidden (46), or dev_public (8). Results on test_hidden and stress_test therefore measure robustness to new perturbations of partly seen base papers, not to unseen papers; the public dev/test comparison is the base-paper-disjoint one. What we transfer from HumanEval ( Chen et al., 2021 ) is the unit-test-per-instance structure rather than its execution semantics. HumanEval ships every problem with its own assertion suite, so a failing candidate names the property it violated instead of merely lowering a scalar; Hallmark ships every entry with its own six declared sub-test outcomes, so a verifier’s error localizes to the verification property it missed. The analogy stops at execution: our sub-tests are metadata checks with declared expected values, not code run against hidden inputs, so there is no pass@ k k analog and no partial credit within an entry. (2021) and Holland et al. (2020) , we provide a datasheet for the Hallmark dataset. Motivation. Hallmark was created to provide a standardized benchmark for evaluating citation hallucination detection tools, motivated by the NeurIPS 2025 incident and subsequent audits. Composition. The public release contains 2,072 BibTeX entries: 826 valid entries scraped from DBLP and arXiv and 1,246 hallucinated entries generated through perturbation, LLM generation, adversarial crafting, and real-world collection across 14 hallucination types. A held-out test_hidden split contains 454 additional entries (210 valid, 244 hallucinated, for 1,036 valid entries in total) (per-entry labels withheld; aggregate label counts in Tab. 5 ), bringing the grand total to 2,526. Split counts: dev_public 1,119 (513 valid / 606 hallucinated), test_public 831 (312 / 519), stress_test 122 (1 / 121). The dev/test/hidden split is deterministic: stratified by hallucination type and tier with seed=8042 (recorded in metadata.json ), so the partition is regenerable from the released corpus. Each entry includes 6 binary sub-test labels. Four evaluation-only splits are released alongside the 2,526-entry benchmark, outside the dev/test/hidden partition: a 60-entry temporal probe, a 448-entry temporal supplement (2024–2025), a 500-entry cross-domain split, and a 341-entry supplement of authentic ChatGPT-generated citations converted from Walters & Wilder (2023) ( Tab. 5 ; the ChatGPT supplement’s construction in § C.6 ). Collection process. Valid entries were scraped from the DBLP API and verified against CrossRef and Semantic Scholar. Hallucinated entries were generated using the methods described in § 3.2 and § A.3 . Preprocessing/Cleaning/Labeling. BibTeX records were normalized to a consistent field ordering. Unicode characters were preserved. Entries were split into dev/test/hidden sets by stratified sampling across hallucination types and tiers (deterministic seed: see Composition ). Labels were assigned deterministically by the generation pipeline; valid entries carry ground-truth sub-test labels derived from database cross-checks. The released corpus is post-relabel: a systematic ground-truth audit ( scripts/relabel_ground_truth.py , commits 32fe9d6 and 1475a2f ) re-resolved entries against live databases and corrected real papers that had been mislabeled HALLUCINATED ; the per-entry flip log is released at results/reviewer_experiments/relabel_flips.json . The two trade off against different deployment costs. A missed fabrication that reaches print is usually the worse error—costlier than a false alarm a reviewer can dismiss—but which error dominates depends on the regime: prevalence (the fraction of entries that are hallucinated), the cost ratio c FN / c FP c_{\text{FN}}/c_{\text{FP}} , and reviewer capacity ( § 4.4 ). We report the false-negative rate = 1 − DR =1-\text{DR} for completeness. Four further metrics summarize a tool in a single number. F1-Hallucination is the harmonic mean of precision and recall on the hallucinated class; it is prevalence-sensitive, so we compare it only within a split. TW-F1 weights each hallucinated entry by its tier (1/2/3), rewarding the detection of harder hallucinations; the ranking of the three tools we swept (DOI-only, GPT-5.1, and bibtex-updater ) held across uniform, linear, and quadratic weightings in the sweep we ran, which we now report with a caveat on the sweep itself ( § B.1 ). Tier-weighted F1. For entries { e i } \{e_{i}\} with tier weights w i ∈ { 1 , 2 , 3 } w_{i}\in\{1,2,3\} , predictions y ^ i \hat{y}_{i} , and labels y i y_{i} : TW-Precision = ∑ i w i ⋅ 𝟏 [ y ^ i = y i = H ] ∑ i w i ⋅ 𝟏 [ y ^ i = H ] , \displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}=\text{H}]}{\sum_{i}w_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_{i}=\text{H}]}, TW-Recall = ∑ i w i ⋅ 𝟏 [ y ^ i = y i = H ] ∑ i w i ⋅ 𝟏 [ y i = H ] , \displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}=\text{H}]}{\sum_{i}w_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}[y_{i}=\text{H}]}, (1) where H denotes the hallucinated class, and TW-F1 is their harmonic mean. B.2 Evaluation implementation All baselines are implemented as Python wrappers conforming to the Hallmark baseline interface. Each wrapper: (1) converts Hallmark BenchmarkEntry objects to the tool’s expected input format, (2) invokes the tool, (3) maps the tool’s output to a Hallmark Prediction with label, confidence, and reason ( Fig. 4 ). Citation BibTeX entry Pre-screening DOI format year bounds name heuristics Tool call API / LLM Metrics DR, FPR, TW-F1, ECE Override direct verdict pass flag shared layer Figure 4: Evaluation pipeline. Each citation entry may pass through an optional shared pre-screening layer (DOI format, year bounds, name heuristics) that emits a final verdict directly; otherwise the entry is forwarded to the tool. The layer is applied to the database-lookup tools, whose reported rows therefore include its verdicts ( § 4.4 ). The LLM verifiers are run without it, so an FPR is comparable across the two blocks only up to that difference. B.2.1 Pre-screening layer specification The pre-screening layer implements four checks that run before external tool invocation: 1. DOI resolution : Normalizes the DOI (strips a URL prefix and, for arXiv DOIs , a version suffix such as v1 , since DataCite registers only the unversioned form) and sends a live HTTP HEAD request to doi.org . Only a 404 or 410 served by doi.org itself flags the entry; a missing or malformed DOI , a network error, a rate limit, a block or a server error yields no verdict, so a transient failure never overrides the tool. 2. Year bounds checking : Flags entries with publication years in the future or before 1900. 1 1 1 Genuinely valid pre-1900 references exist, of course (classical mathematics and physics citations); the bound is a corpus-tuned heuristic—no entry pool in the benchmark carries a publication year before 2021—so deployments verifying older literature should relax or drop this check. 3. Author name heuristics : Detects common placeholder patterns (“John Doe,” “A. Author,” single-word author names, repeated names). 4. Capitalized-token placeholders : Flags author fields built from placeholder tokens that the previous check misses: a capitalized word followed by digits ( Smith2 ), a field made only of initials, or one where more than half the tokens are all-uppercase words. Pre-screening results are tagged with [Pre-screening override] in the reason string to maintain transparency about which detections come from the pre-screening layer vs. the external tool. B.2.2 HaRC and verify-citations: why neither is in the main table We attempted HaRC ( HaRC Contributors, 2024 ) and verify-citations ( verify-citations Contributors, 2025 ) as full-coverage baselines and could not obtain a verdict from either at benchmark scale. The binding constraint for HaRC is not Semantic Scholar throttling but harcx ’s own lookup path, which queries Google Scholar through scholarly : Scholar blocks the client, the library retries rather than failing, and under the -q --threshold 0.75 invocation our wrapper uses even a single-entry .bib does not complete within 150 s. Dropping -q returns in seconds but emits no verdict line at all, exit 0. An authenticated S2 key (1 RPS, 5-hour budget) does not change either behavior, so 0 of 1,119 entries are actually checked by harcx . verify-citations exhibits the same throughput pathology and we never ran it over a full split; the released artifact for it covers a 500-entry sample. In both cases whatever verdicts a run does produce come from the shared pre-screening layer ( § B.2.1 ) rather than from the tool, so the numbers would not measure what the row claims to measure. Both tools are therefore excluded from Tab. 1 . The one keyed HaRC artifact we release ( harc_with_s2key_dev_public.json ) records DR = 0.209, FPR = 0.045, F1 = 0.335 over all 1,119 entries, but every one of those verdicts is a pre-screening verdict, and the file is scored against the pre-relabel ground truth (633 hallucinated / 486 valid, against the current 606/513), so it is comparable to no other number in this paper and we register it as known-stale rather than report it as a baseline. The OpenAI agentic and the Anthropic agentic- bibtex-updater runs are the variants reported in the paper; numbers come from data/v1.0/baseline_results/llm_agentic_btu_sonnet_4_6_dev_public.json and the corresponding OpenAI checkpoint. Tools are exposed via the provider’s native function-calling interface with tool_choice="auto" : the model may answer without any tool call, and we track the parametric-vs-tool split per prediction. Up to MAX_TOOL_CALLS=5 invocations per entry, plus one final verdict turn. If the cap is exceeded without a verdict, the entry is assigned UNCERTAIN@0.5 . The multi-source suite comprises 4 tools, each returning {authors, title, venue, year, doi} with fields truncated to 500 characters: • resolve_doi(doi) : CrossRef DOI resolution ( api.crossref.org/works/{doi} ). • search_crossref(query, limit=5) : CrossRef bibliographic search (max 20 results). • search_openalex(query, limit=5) : OpenAlex polite pool (max 25). • search_arxiv(query, limit=5) : arXiv Atom API (max 20). All HTTP requests use a HALLMARK/1.0 User-Agent and 10–15 s timeouts; tool exceptions are retried with exponential backoff (up to 3 retries, base delay 1 s). The bibtex-updater -as-tool variant exposes a single tool, verify_with_bibtex_updater(bibtex) , that invokes the bibtex-check CLI with --rate-limit 120 --academic-only , returning {status, confidence, mismatched_fields, api_sources, errors} . Subprocess timeout: 180 s. External-database access. The three database-backed endpoints— DOI -only, standalone bibtex-updater , and the agentic suite—query five external services: CrossRef ( api.crossref.org , polite pool, no key), OpenAlex (polite pool, identified by a mailto contact), Semantic Scholar (the graph/v1 paper-lookup API, accessed with an S2_API_KEY ), the arXiv Atom API, and DBLP. Semantic Scholar resolves a citation by matching its title, authors, venue, and identifiers against the S2 corpus and returning the canonical record—or none—which is the evidence bibtex-updater weighs to confirm or flag an entry. Semantic Scholar requires an API key for reliable programmatic access: the free key moves requests off the throttled shared public pool onto a dedicated per-key rate limit. The key affects only throughput and reliability: an unauthenticated re-run hits the shared limit (frequent HTTP 429) and resolves the corpus more slowly, but queries the same records and returns the same matches, so it does not change the reported verdicts. The main-run numbers were queried on 2026-05-04/05. Agentic tool results are replayable offline from the released sha256 -keyed tool-cache (mechanics under Tool-call caching below), so the agentic baselines reproduce without live calls. The DOI -only and standalone- bibtex-updater endpoints have no such cache, so their numbers are dated snapshots of the corresponding databases. System prompts. C.5 Validity checks C.5.1 Core subset analysis Scaling the hallucinated pool does not distort the evaluation signals. The pool grew out of 114 seed hallucinations (71 in dev, 43 in test) through generation across all main hallucination types, and to check the scaling we re-evaluated all baselines on the core subset containing only the 114 seed hallucinations alongside the valid entries. This check was performed on an earlier corpus snapshot: 816 hallucinated (453 dev / 363 test) and 720 valid public entries at the time; it predates the final corpus expansion and the ground-truth relabel, and the released corpus counts are in Tab. 5 . On that snapshot, aggregate metrics differ by less than 2% between the core subset and the full pool across all baselines: detection rate changes by at most 0.015, F1 by at most 0.02, and tier-weighted F1 by at most 0.018. Per-type rankings are preserved: no baseline changes rank on any type. We conclude that the scaled entries are consistent with the seed distribution and do not introduce systematic bias. C.5.2 Synthetic vs. real-world representativeness We can detect no surface-feature artifact separating synthetic from real hallucinations, but we cannot yet claim the two are equivalent. For nine dev_public types with both real and synthetic entries, per-type Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests on title length, author count, and year fail to reject the null for 6/9 ( p > 0.05 p>0.05 ); the three divergent types differ mainly in author count (real entries average 2–3 authors, synthetic 3–4). This is not evidence of equivalence: failing to reject a null is not accepting it, the real set has only ∼ 5 {\sim}5 – 20 20 entries per type (so the KS test is badly underpowered), and these three surface features are exactly the ones our shortcut analysis ( § C.5.4 ) shows carry little signal (a logistic regression trained on those features beats the majority-class baseline—always predicting the more frequent label—by only 4.6 4.6 pp accuracy, below the 5 5 pp margin we treat as evidence of an exploitable shortcut). The honest reading is narrow: no artifact strong enough to clear the leakage threshold appears, but distributional and semantic equivalence remains untested at these sample sizes. That is the central open validity question, which would need an equivalence test on a much larger real set, not a null-hypothesis test that cannot tell “similar” from “insufficient data”. Tool detection is comparable across strata (GPT-5.1: 0.66 0.66 on LLM -generated vs. 0.85 0.85 on perturbations, with Sonnet 4.6 the same direction), consistent with LLM -generated entries being intrinsically harder rather than with self-recognition. This comparability supports perturbations as controlled diagnostics and motivates expanding the 108-entry real-world set. Appendix D Failure mode (i): agentic aggregation A deterministic re-aggregation over three of the harness’s four databases confirms the lever: holding sources and matcher fixed, switching from any-no-match flagging (flag if any single database returns no match) to consensus flagging (flag only when all queried databases agree the entry is absent) cuts FPR from 0.73 0.73 to 0.05 0.05 (a ∼ 15 × {\sim}15{\times} reduction) at a recall cost on metadata-corruption types, which pairing consensus with contradiction checks— bibtex-updater ’s design—recovers ( Tab. 21 ). So failure mode (i) characterizes this common retrieval-augmented pattern, not every agentic design. Tab. 20 names failure mode (i)—the false-positive inflation of agentic, retrieval-augmented verifiers ( Tab. 1 ). Here we isolate its mechanism: the inflation is a property of the aggregation rule that turns multi-database lookups into a verdict, with the base model held fixed, and Tab. 21 quantifies the fix. Cross-source aggregation on the agentic databases. Failure mode (i) attributes the agentic harness’s inflated FPR to its emergent any-no-match disposition; we isolate that aggregation rule directly. Holding the databases and the matcher fixed, we query three of the harness’s four databases—CrossRef, OpenAlex, and arXiv—for every dev_public entry, count a source as confirming when it returns a record whose title matches the entry ( ≥ 0.85 \geq 0.85 similarity), and score three rules over identical evidence. Any-no-match (flag if any source misses) reaches FPR 0.729 0.729 at DR 0.835 0.835 ; consensus (flag only if all three miss) cuts FPR to 0.049 0.049 —roughly 15 × 15{\times} lower—at DR 0.251 0.251 ; majority (flag if fewer than half confirm) falls between, at FPR 0.201 0.201 / DR 0.417 0.417 . The FPR reduction is therefore a property of the aggregation rule, not the model or the sources. E.2 Selective prediction and the Coverage column Structural precision (from the main text). bibtex-updater ’s low false-positive rate is structural : it comes from conservative matching, not from abstention. It abstains on the ∼ 14 % {\sim}14\% of entries it cannot back with a record; on the entries it commits to, its FPR is essentially unchanged while its detection rate increases ( Tab. 25 ); abstention raises recall and leaves precision unchanged. Because its low FPR does not depend on abstention, bibtex-updater serves as the precision-anchored reference against which the LLMs ’ abstention-driven gains are measured, and the reason we keep it out of the ranking ( § G.2 ). Because DR and FPR are prevalence-independent, we compare on them, and on F1 only within a split. The Coverage column of Tab. 1 reports, for each tool, the fraction of entries on which it commits to a VALID/HALLUCINATED verdict rather than abstaining (UNCERTAIN). E.3 Regime-conditional deployment guidance When to prefer recall. The operating point is set by three quantities: prevalence, the cost ratio c FN / c FP c_{\text{FN}}/c_{\text{FP}} of a missed fabrication versus a false alarm, and reviewer capacity. FPR -ranking holds in the reviewer-bound venue-audit regime—low prevalence, finite reviewer attention, a false alarm cheap to dismiss—where precision is the limiting factor. When a missed fabrication is far costlier than triaging extra flags, or when a later human-review stage filters out the false alarms the tool raises, recall plus human triage dominates and a high-recall tool (an agentic harness, or DeepSeek-V3.2) is preferable despite its FPR . The two takeaways—“rank on FPR ” and “a missed hallucination is the worse error”—are not in tension; they name different aspects of the ( prevalence , c FN / c FP , capacity ) (\text{prevalence},c_{\text{FN}}/c_{\text{FP}},\text{capacity}) space, which Tab. 26 resolves into tool choices. The two later-cutoff Anthropic models (Sonnet 4.6, Opus 4.7) sharpen the point: their inferred cutoffs (Aug 2025, Oct 2025) are not radically later than Qwen3-235B’s, yet their FPR shifts on the supplement are an order of magnitude smaller. Cutoff recency is therefore necessary but insufficient; pipeline-level differences ( Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) for abstention, “I don’t know” calibration) appear to dominate the temporal-robustness story. These results establish that LLM citation verification leans heavily on parametric knowledge with a graded temporal boundary near the training cutoff: most models fall into the “flag everything unfamiliar” failure mode for post-cutoff papers. But the partial-to-full resistance from Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5.4, and the two Anthropic models shows this is not a hard structural limit of parametric verification: training-pipeline interventions (post-training calibration, abstention RLHF ) can shift the trade-off without retrieval augmentation, even at comparable training cutoffs. We therefore do not attribute their resistance to calibration on this supplement alone: the recall probe and the third-provider control ( § F.4 ) point toward calibration for Opus 4.7 without excluding recall, and separating the two fully would require post-cutoff valid entries the models provably never saw, which the current supplement cannot guarantee. The temporal results above establish H1 : LLMs do not know their own cutoff; post-cutoff FPR rises sharply because the model treats unfamiliar papers as suspicious. A separate hypothesis remains untested: H2 , that LLMs can route post-cutoff citations to UNCERTAIN if explicitly reminded of the cutoff . H1 and H2 are orthogonal: H1 failing does not imply H2 must fail (the model may have latent metacognitive capacity that the default prompt does not elicit), and H2 failing would upgrade H1 from “epistemic miscalibration” to “structural blindness to temporal uncertainty.” Prompt addendum. Appendix G bibtex-updater: the co-designed reference tool This section collects everything on bibtex-updater , the co-designed reference tool: its implementation ( § G.1 ), its benchmark evaluation and the co-design-bias controls ( § G.2 ), and a tool-augmented LLM baseline ( § G.2 ). G.1 Implementation We additionally describe bibtex-updater , a co-developed open-source citation verification tool designed for deployment by venues, reviewers, and authors: not to optimize benchmark scores, but to provide reliable, automated checking that integrates into existing publication workflows. G.1.1 Design goals Three practical requirements drive the design: 1. Zero human effort. Verification must be fully automated: no manual review, no prompt engineering, no LLM inference costs. This rules out approaches requiring human-in-the-loop validation or expensive API calls to language models. 2. Workflow integration. The tool must integrate into existing pipelines: CI/CD (GitHub Actions), pre-commit hooks, Overleaf builds, and one-off command-line checks. A tool that requires a separate platform or manual invocation will not be adopted. 3. Graceful degradation. When APIs are unavailable or rate-limited, the tool should return partial results rather than fail silently. Venues processing hundreds of submissions cannot tolerate flaky infrastructure. G.1.2 Verification pipeline bibtex-updater implements a multi-stage pipeline that processes each BibTeX entry through increasingly expensive checks: Pre-API validation (zero cost). Before any network calls, the tool checks for syntactic red flags: DOIs that fail to resolve (HEAD request to doi.org , counting only doi.org ’s own rejection as non-resolution—a publisher-side block or bot-mitigation status reached after a successful redirect is not evidence the DOI is absent, and a versioned arXiv DOI is normalized to its unversioned form first), future publication years, implausible dates ( < 1800 <1800 ), and malformed fields. These cheap checks catch Tier 1 hallucinations without API overhead. Multi-source lookup. The tool queries Crossref, DBLP, and Semantic Scholar using title and first-author search, and resolves arXiv IDs against the arXiv Atom API for preprint-specific consistency checks. Each source returns candidate records that are scored using a weighted combination of fuzzy title matching (70%, token-sort ratio) and author Jaccard similarity (30%). The best-scoring candidate across all sources is selected for field-by-field comparison. Post-match analysis. Once a candidate is identified, the tool compares DOI , title, authors, year, and venue against the input entry. Venue comparison uses alias-aware matching for 17 major ML/AI venues (e.g., NeurIPS/NIPS, ICML, ICLR, CVPR), so that name variations within that set do not trigger false positives; ISO-4 journal abbreviations outside it ( ACM Trans. Graph. , Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. U.S.A. ) fall below the match threshold and were a substantial false-positive source in the version evaluated here. A dedicated preprint detection stage queries Semantic Scholar to identify entries that claim venue publication when only an arXiv preprint exists. Status assignment. Each entry receives one status code. Thirteen occur on dev_public : verified (442), unconfirmed (147), swapped_authors (146), partial_match (85), venue_mismatch (66), not_found (52), doi_not_found (46), doi_mismatch (36), future_date (30), arxiv_id_mismatch (18), year_mismatch (17), title_mismatch (14) and given_name_substitution (13). The 147 unconfirmed verdicts are where most of the tool’s abstention sits. The HALLMARK wrapper maps these to binary labels and confidence scores for benchmark evaluation. G.1.3 Deployment modes The tool supports three deployment scenarios: CI/CD integration ( --strict flag exits with nonzero code on detection, gating submission workflows), pre-commit hooks (validates .bib files on every commit), and batch processing (concurrent workers with rate limiting for venue-scale use). For venue-scale deployment, we recommend a two-stage workflow: run bibtex-updater to flag suspicious citations, then manual review of high-confidence flags (confidence ≥ 0.7 \geq 0.7 ); at a 2 % 2\% base rate expect roughly one true hallucination per four flags, and read that as the optimistic end—at 1 % 1\% it is one per seven and at 0.5 % 0.5\% one per thirteen, and the prevalence we measured on real submissions was indistinguishable from zero ( §§ E.1 and 5 ). At NeurIPS scale (approximately 10,000 submissions, approximately 50 references each) that is half a million references. At the throughput we measure with keys, credentials and a 20-per-minute pace ( 2.4 2.4 s per entry) eight workers need roughly 42 42 hours; at the wrapper’s older rate limit ( 5.7 5.7 s per entry) roughly 99 99 . The tool requires no GPU or LLM API keys and is MIT-licensed; code is publicly available at https://github.com/rpatrik96/hallmark . Appendix H Robustness ablations We report four ablations that probe whether the precision-ceiling finding is an artifact of a single design choice—a prompt, a threshold, an input field, or a single annotator—alongside the selective-prediction analysis of § E.2 . We read all four as robustness evidence for the ranking , not as tuning for best numbers: the tool ranking is what transfers across the regimes these ablations vary ( § 4.3 )—it does not survive a change in what counts as a hallucination, which § H.1 measures separately—so we test its invariance to phrasing ( § H.2 ), operating point ( § H.4 ), input format ( § H.5 ), and rater ( § H.6 ). H.1 What counts as a hallucination changes the ranking Four of the fourteen types label entries whose cited work exists. wrong_venue assigns a real paper to the wrong conference, preprint_as_published cites a real preprint as though it appeared at a venue, partial_author_list truncates a real author list without marking it, and arxiv_version_mismatch cites a real preprint with the wrong venue and a shifted year. In each case the reference points at a work a reader can find; what is wrong is the description. The other ten types cover works that do not exist. A verifier that flags all fourteen is doing two jobs, and the benchmark scores them as one. The distinction is not marginal. On test_public the four modes are 139 of 519 hallucinated entries ( 26.8 % 26.8\% ), and on dev_public 158 of 606 ( 26.1 % 26.1\% ). They are not confined to stress_test , and the stress split is 61.5 % 61.5\% real-paper modes in its own right. We re-score all 20 tool configurations three ways ( Tab. 42 ): as shipped; with the four modes folded out of the evaluation set entirely; and with them scored as false accusations, so a flag on one counts against the tool. The ranking is stable to neither. Folding them out moves 12 of 20 configurations in rank; scoring them as false accusations moves 16 of 20 and changes the leader. Appendix I Extended limitations The seven paragraphs below are the full-length limitations discussion that § 5 ranks and compresses. Dataset scale and coverage. The benchmark is small relative to the full diversity of citation hallucinations. At 2,526 entries, most types on dev_public and test_public clear ≥ 30 \geq 30 instances—enough for meaningful per-type comparison—but a few fall below it, and test_hidden does not reach the floor at all ( § B.1 ). Prevalence differs across splits (54.2–62.5%), so we emphasize the prevalence-independent metrics ( DR , FPR ). We cover English-language BibTeX from five ML venues (2021–2023); the taxonomy and sub-tests are designed to be domain-agnostic but validated mainly in that regime. A 500-entry cross-domain split (PubMed/bioRxiv plus non-ML CS venues), together with a recency-matched, canonically-resolved rebuild, separates domain from post-cutoff recency out of regime ( § G.2 ). The separation reframes the result: the released split’s apparent cross-domain FPR rise is mostly a recency artifact—the LLMs flag 2026 dates as impossible—and once entries are pre-cutoff the residual domain effect is negative or small for the calibrated verifiers, large only for a single recall-aggressive model that already over-flags in its own regime ( Tab. 39 ). For bibtex-updater , canonical metadata leaves the FPR at its in-domain level while coverage falls: out of domain the cost is coverage, not precision. Two failure modes stay specific to biomedical data—provider safety filters block ∼ 3 % {\sim}3\% of valid biomedical citations for the Anthropic models, distinct from any verdict error—while humanities and non-English settings remain future work. Just over half the valid entries lack a DOI ( 53.0 % 53.0\% on dev_public , 55.4 % 55.4\% on test_public )—many ML papers are never formally published, and DBLP records for ICML, ICLR and NeurIPS carry no DOI at all—which raises the FPR of verifiers that treat a missing identifier as suspicious. Synthetic–real gap. Most hallucinated entries are perturbation-generated, and the real-world anchor is thin. The benchmark holds 108 real-world entries (plus 280 LLM -generated), type-skewed (55% plausible_fabrication ), with 15 compound-failure entries that involved judgment calls without human inter-annotator agreement. We report an automated three-rater reliability proxy (Fleiss’ κ = 0.24 \kappa=0.24 , fair inter-rater agreement; § H.6 ) that corroborates the relabel audit but does not substitute for human IAA , which remains future work. Expanding real-world coverage from retraction databases is a priority. Temporal fragility and the calibration question. LLM verifiers carry an implicit training-cutoff bias that we can only partly disentangle. § 4.4 measures the post-cutoff FPR rise and the later-cutoff resistance; a recall probe and a non-Anthropic late-cutoff control point away from pure memorization for Opus 4.7 (and only partly for Sonnet 4.6), but the recall measure is model-derived and residual confounds—system-prompt handling, default temperature, native-vs-OpenRouter routing—remain. We therefore read the resistance as a descriptive signal, not a causal attribution. Controls and residual gaps. Three controls probe the calibration-versus-contamination question, none decisive ( § F.4 ). (i) A recall probe on valid 2024–2025 papers is consistent with calibration over memorization for Opus 4.7 (it accepts 84 % 84\% of papers it cannot recall), while Sonnet 4.6’s lower FPR is partly recall-driven. (ii) A third-provider late-cutoff control (DeepSeek-V4-Pro) also holds its post-cutoff FPR low relative to the over-flagging cohort ( 0.36 0.36 vs. 0.93 0.93 for GPT-5.1 on the same n = 300 n{=}300 subsample, at full coverage), though its cutoff matches rather than exceeds the Anthropic pair’s ( § F.4 ). (iii) GPT-5.1’s run-to-run variance is small ( F1 std 0.002 0.002 over three runs), so single-run rankings seem to be stable to sampling noise, at least for some models. The open gaps: the cross-provider control is a single third-party model, a native-API replication of the Anthropic runs is outstanding, and contamination is reduced but not fully excluded even for Opus 4.7. Compute and token budget. Compute caps the coverage of reasoning-mode and extended-thinking variants. Reasoning models cost far more per entry (DeepSeek-R1 at a median ∼ 19 {\sim}19 s vs. 1 1 – 10 10 s, Tab. 19 ), so we did not run extended-thinking variants on the full benchmark ( Fig. 7 maps where their JSON-output contract holds), the agentic harness is capped at five tool calls per entry, and HaRC/verify-citations are excluded because neither produces its own verdicts at benchmark scale ( § B.2.2 ). Regime conditionality and co-design. The “ FPR decides” framing is calibrated to the reviewer-bound venue-audit regime: low prevalence, finite reviewer attention, a false alarm cheap to dismiss. The PPV ranking is itself prevalence-invariant—sweeping prevalence over 1 1 – 5 % 5\% does not reorder the tools ( Tab. 24 )—so what changes across regimes is whether any verifier reaches a usable absolute precision and the cost asymmetry c FN / c FP c_{\text{FN}}/c_{\text{FP}} ; under high prevalence or a high cost of a missed fabrication, recall and human triage regain primacy. Separately, bibtex-updater and the taxonomy were developed in parallel, so the taxonomy reflects fundamental verification steps but the construct-overfitting risk is non-zero; we report it as a co-designed reference ( § G.2 ). A second co-design channel runs through the valid-entry inclusion rule, and it is arguably stronger than the taxonomy: § 3.2 admits a valid entry only after confirming DOI resolution, title existence in at least two databases, and author–venue consistency, so every valid entry is database-confirmable by construction, and FPR —the axis we rank on—is computed on exactly that half. The rule therefore favors database-lookup architectures structurally, independently of how the taxonomy was derived. What bounds the effect is that it does not decide the outcome: an independent parametric verifier matches it: Gemini 2.5 Pro’s FPR ( .050 .050 ) is level with the co-designed tool’s ( .051 .051 ). Whether rankings on synthetic hallucinations predict performance on real errors remains open ( Hardt, 2026 ) ; the 108 real-world entries are an initial signal, and the codebase tracks the number of times the dev split has been evaluated against during development, an adaptive-data-analysis hygiene measure that bounds the overfitting introduced by repeatedly reusing the same held-out set ( Dwork et al., 2015 ) . Source availability is a second measurement condition. Every number a retrieval-dependent verifier produces is conditional on whether the databases answered, and we report none of that conditioning per row. It is not a small effect. Holding tool and split fixed and correcting only the source conditions—keys, credentials and a 20-per-minute pace—moved the fraction of entries with at least one lookup that never completed from 53.7 % 53.7\% to 10.0 % 10.0\% , and the run got faster rather than slower, 2.4 2.4 s per entry against 5.7 5.7 : a starved source turns into a retry storm that costs more than the wait it avoids. On a 119-entry probe holding model and prompt fixed, 12 % 12\% of flags cleared with arXiv starved against 24 % 24\% with it answering: the same verifier on the same entries, half the corrections. Nothing in this paper controls for it: our rows were collected under whatever the sources were doing that day, which bounds how exactly any of them can be reproduced, and we flag it as an evaluation condition that citation-verification benchmarks, ours included, do not yet report. Endpoint drift and reproducibility. Several ablations and the two Anthropic risk–coverage curves were collected against the OpenRouter API on a dated snapshot that does not reproduce the main-run aggregates. The Anthropic endpoints drift over time, and for these two models the drift is large enough to dominate several of the effects we measure. Two independently collected later runs agree on its direction and rough size: an OpenRouter snapshot puts Opus 4.7 at 0.072 → 0.162 0.072{\to}0.162 and Sonnet 4.6 at 0.127 → 0.165 0.127{\to}0.165 , and the 2026-09-02 re-run lands at 0.154 0.154 and 0.161 0.161 ( Tab. 29 ). Both roughly double the May FPR under a fixed model ID, prompt, split, temperature and seed. That re-run also persists per-entry predictions, which the original main run did not, so the Sonnet/Opus coverage cells and dev confidence intervals are now recoverable and are reported rather than marked “n/a”. Our convention follows from the size of the drift rather than from a preference for either collection. Cross-sectional comparisons take the latest run, so Tab. 1 reports 2026-09-02 for these two rows. Any comparison whose quantity is a difference stays inside one collection, because a 13 13 pp endpoint shift swamps the differences at stake: the post-cutoff shift in Tab. 28 is under 1 1 pp for both models, so those rows remain wholly on May, and the agentic-inflation ratio of failure mode (i) is likewise quoted within May. Mixing the two would not merely add noise, it would report drift as a finding—pairing a September 21–23 FPR with a May 24–25 one yields − 4.1 -4.1 and − 8.1 -8.1 pp, i.e. apparent improvement on post-cutoff papers. Rankings are the more robust reading in any case, since drift shifts absolute FPR in the same direction across models while largely preserving their order (cross-regime ranking at Spearman ρ = 0.90 \rho{=}0.90 under prompt perturbation, § H.2 ). Within-run deltas (prompt-variant, field- LOO , rater-agreement) difference conditions measured against the same endpoint on the same day and so survive the drift. Appendix J Broader impact and deployment safeguards Hallmark supports scientific integrity by enabling systematic comparison of citation-verification tools, informing venue deployment as LLM -assisted writing becomes standard. A taxonomy of hallucination types could theoretically aid adversaries, but the defensive value of understanding these types outweighs the risk; the taxonomy is derived from publicly documented incidents, not novel attack research. The benchmark is open-source (MIT) and GPU-free, running on a single CPU regardless of institutional resources. Intended use, and two uses we advise against. Hallmark is a diagnostic for tool builders and venue operators: it measures which verifier fails on which citation error, and at what precision cost. It is not built for autonomous rejection of submissions, nor for screening authors for research misconduct, and we advise against both. Both inferences require establishing intent, which no verifier in this evaluation attempts and no metadata check can support: a flag means the metadata did not resolve against the databases queried, not that an author fabricated anything. Our own measurements are the argument against automating the decision. At a venue-realistic ∼ 2 % {\sim}2\% base rate the best verifiers in the cohort catch one true hallucination per 6–10 flags and the most aggressive fewer than one in 35 ( Tab. 23 ), so no operating point we measured would make an automated gate defensible. Deployment safeguards. Three safeguards follow from those measurements rather than from principle. A human decides: a verifier’s output routes an entry to a reviewer for checking and never stands as a verdict on the citation. A flag arrives with its evidence—which databases were queried and what each returned—so that an author can rebut it without reverse-engineering the tool. And an appeal route exists and is cheap: an author who supplies a resolving identifier or a canonical database record clears the flag without adjudication, which is the correct outcome for the recency and indexing failures below, where the tool is wrong and the citation is fine. Who a false positive lands on. False alarms are not distributed evenly across authors, and the unevenness is measurable rather than speculative. Averaged over the eighteen verifiers with released per-entry predictions, FPR orders the five venues identically on both public splits—ICML 0.44 0.44 , ICLR 0.36 0.36 , NeurIPS 0.32 0.32 , AAAI 0.13 0.13 , CVPR 0.09 0.09 —and the mean pairwise Spearman correlation between verifiers’ per-venue rates is + 0.56 +0.56 , so the burden is a property of the corpus rather than of any one tool. What the ordering tracks is bibliographic completeness, not venue standing: no valid ICML, ICLR or NeurIPS entry in the public splits carries a DOI , against every entry at CVPR and AAAI. Per-verifier spread across those venues runs from 1.3 1.3 pp for Gemini 2.5 Pro and 6.3 6.3 pp for bibtex-updater to 69.5 69.5 pp for GPT-5.1 and 93.9 93.9 pp for the agentic harness, so a low- FPR verifier also buys uniformity, while an aggressive operating point concentrates its errors on whoever is least well indexed. Publication recency behaves as a cliff rather than a gradient: within 2021–2023 no verifier’s year-to-year spread exceeds 19 19 pp, but on the 35 real February-2026 arXiv preprints in the corpus—post-cutoff for every model evaluated—all eighteen verifiers over-flag and fifteen reach FPR 1.000 1.000 , and on the 448-entry 2024–2025 supplement all twelve LLMs are worse on 2025 than on 2024 ( §§ 4.4 and F.1 ). The authors most exposed are therefore those publishing recent work and work whose venue records carry no resolvable identifier, which is close to the inverse of who can most easily absorb the cost. A venue that deploys a verifier without first measuring its own subgroup false-positive rates will impose this burden without observing it. Scope of the evidence. The conclusions here are established on English-language BibTeX from five ML venues (2021–2023), extended by a 500-entry cross-domain split covering biomedical and non-ML computer-science sources and by an authentic- LLM -output supplement spanning 42 multidisciplinary topics ( §§ G.2 and C.6 ). They are not established for non-English citation practices, for humanities conventions where monographs and archival sources dominate, or for fields whose canonical identifiers differ from the DOI /arXiv pair these tools query. Deploying a verifier in those settings requires re-measuring its false-positive rate there first, and this benchmark does not license the transfer.
